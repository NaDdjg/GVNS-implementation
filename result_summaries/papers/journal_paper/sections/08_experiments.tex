\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
Experiments were conducted to evaluate both the proposed GVNS+ framework and the learning-guided adaptive mechanisms introduced in this work. The objectives are twofold. First, we assess the optimization capability and scalability of the enhanced GVNS baseline on both classical synchronized benchmarks and newly generated large-scale VRPTW-S instances. Second, we investigate the impact of the proposed learning components, including adaptive operator selection, local-search strategy selection, and PPO-based parameter control.

The experimental study uses three benchmark families. The first two correspond to the synchronized benchmark sets of Bredström and Rönnqvist and Liu et al., which constitute standard reference datasets for the VRPTW-S and are used for the validation of the GVNS+ framework in comparison to the literature. The third benchmark family consists of the large-scale synchronized Solomon-derived instances introduced in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}, covering problem sizes ranging from 50 to 400 customers.

For all learning-based experiments, the generated benchmark instances were divided into development and evaluation subsets. For each problem size among 50, 100, and 200 customers, 18 instances were reserved exclusively for testing, while the remaining instances were used for data collection, model training, and validation. The 400-customer instances were never used during training and serve exclusively as an out-of-distribution evaluation set to assess generalization capabilities.

Unless stated otherwise, each configuration was executed independently 10 times. Reported results correspond to averages over these runs. The stopping criteria combine a maximum runtime limit and a stagnation threshold based on the number of consecutive non-improving iterations.

\subsection{Operator selection}
\label{sec:operator_selection}

This experiment evaluates the ability of reinforcement learning to dynamically select neighborhood operators during the GVNS+ search process. Two learning approaches are considered: a tabular Q-learning agent and a Deep Q-Network (DQN). Both models operate at the neighborhood-selection level and replace the fixed operator ordering traditionally used within the GVNS framework.

All experiments were conducted on the generated synchronized Solomon benchmark instances introduced previously. Each instance was executed independently ten times and average results are reported. Performance is measured relative to the GVNS+ baseline using the percentage gap in objective value and the runtime ratio.

Table~\ref{tab:qlearning_summary} summarizes the performance of the Q-learning controller compared with GVNS+.

\input{../tables/qlearning_summary.tex}

The results indicate that tabular Q-learning is capable of improving neighborhood selection on small and medium-scale instances. For 50-customer problems, the learned policy consistently identifies beneficial operator choices and achieves several improvements over the baseline while maintaining a runtime comparable to GVNS+. Similar behavior is observed on 100-customer instances, where some runs obtain substantial gains reaching nearly 4\%.

Since information cannot be generalized across similar states, many state-action pairs remain insufficiently explored and the learned policy becomes increasingly sparse.

\subsection{FI/BI selection}
This section investigates a different source of adaptivity inside GVNS+: the choice of the local-search evaluation strategy. Instead of systematically applying the First Improvement (FI) policy used in GVNS+, a supervised learning model is employed to dynamically select between FI and Best Improvement (BI) according to the current search context.

The objective is to determine whether machine learning can learn when rapid intensification through FI is preferable and when the more exhaustive exploration provided by BI is likely to produce higher-quality improvements.

Before conducting the complete benchmark evaluation, preliminary experiments were performed on a reduced subset of instances in order to analyse the online behaviour of the learned policy when embedded inside the GVNS+ framework.

The first observation is that the selector develops a clear non-random preference between FI and BI. On the validation subset, BI was selected in approximately 66\% of decisions, while FI accounted for the remaining 34\%. This indicates that the model successfully captures structural differences between both strategies rather than oscillating randomly between them.

Furthermore, the learned policy consistently outperformed both pure-FI and pure-BI baselines on this subset, supporting the hypothesis that dynamically combining the two strategies is more effective than committing statically to a single search mode throughout the optimization process.

A larger-scale evaluation was then conducted on the complete benchmark set. Each instance was executed five times and compared against GVNS+, which uses a pure FI strategy.

Table~\ref{tab:bf_selector_summary} summarizes the global performance of the BF Selector across all benchmark families.
\input{../tables/bf_selector_summary.tex}

On small and medium-scale instances, the BF Selector achieves globally competitive but slightly inferior performance compared with GVNS+. The average gaps remain relatively small, reaching approximately +1.32\% on 50-customer instances and +0.81\% on 100-customer instances. While several instances exhibit meaningful improvements, some degradations are also observed, particularly on smaller problems where the worst gap exceeds +9\%.

Overall, the learned policy remains reasonably stable but does not consistently improve the search process. Improvements and degradations remain relatively balanced across the benchmark, suggesting that the selector successfully captures useful patterns but still struggles to identify the most beneficial search mode in every situation.

This behaviour can largely be explained by the architecture itself. The classifier component is highly effective at identifying moves with a high probability of success, naturally favouring FI decisions and promoting rapid local improvements. However, on small and medium search spaces, the regression component remains less reliable when estimating the actual magnitude of potential gains. Since the difference between FI and BI outcomes is often limited at this scale, prediction noise can easily bias the decision score toward overly greedy behaviour.

As a consequence, the search tends to intensify too early and may miss deeper improvements that would require more extensive BI exploration. The selector therefore behaves as a more directed but occasionally myopic version of GVNS+.

From a computational perspective, runtime remains close to the baseline. In several cases, the preference for FI leads to faster convergence, while in others additional exploratory phases slightly increase execution time. Overall, the adaptive mechanism introduces only limited computational overhead.

On larger benchmark instances, the behaviour becomes more balanced and informative. Average gaps progressively decrease to approximately +0.53\% on 200-customer instances and +0.28\% on 400-customer instances. At the same time, the selector achieves several meaningful improvements while keeping degradations relatively controlled.

This improvement can be explained by the richer learning signals provided by larger search spaces. As problem complexity increases, gain variability also increases, making the regression task easier. The model can therefore more effectively distinguish situations where exhaustive BI exploration is worthwhile from situations where FI remains sufficient.

Nevertheless, the policy remains heterogeneous. Since decisions are based on predicted gains rather than exact evaluations, prediction errors may still lead to suboptimal FI/BI choices. On large instances, such early mistakes can significantly influence the remainder of the search trajectory and occasionally produce noticeable degradations. 

Runtime behaviour remains very close to GVNS+, confirming that the adaptive mechanism reallocates the search effort rather than increasing computational complexity.

To better understand the internal behaviour of the learned strategy selector, its decisions were analysed on instance \texttt{C107\_100\_10}, where the adaptive policy clearly outperformed GVNS+.

Figure~\ref{fig:bf_selector_behavior} presents the overall frequency of FI and BI decisions during the optimization process, and illustrates their temporal evolution throughout local-search iterations.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{../figures/bf_frequency.png}
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{../figures/bf_evolution.png}

The selector chooses BI in approximately 79\% of decisions, confirming a strong preference for deeper exploration whenever the model predicts sufficiently valuable improvements. FI decisions account for only a small fraction of calls and occur primarily during the early search stages.

The temporal evolution further highlights this behaviour. During the initial search phase, FI dominates because rapid inexpensive improvements are abundant. As the search progresses and improving moves become rarer, the selector gradually shifts toward BI, whose exhaustive neighbourhood exploration becomes more effective at identifying the remaining high-quality moves.

This behaviour illustrates a meaningful exploration–exploitation trade-off learned by the model. Rather than statically committing to a single local-search strategy, the selector adapts its behaviour according to the current optimization context and the estimated value of deeper exploration.

\subsection{Hybrid models}
The previous experiments investigated reinforcement learning and supervised learning independently. This section evaluates whether combining both paradigms can further improve the adaptive capabilities of GVNS+.

The objective of these hybrid models is not to replace one learning mechanism with another, but rather to exploit their complementary strengths. Reinforcement learning is responsible for selecting neighbourhood operators, while the supervised BF selector independently determines whether the selected neighbourhood should be explored using FI or BI.

The resulting framework therefore combines two adaptive decision layers: adaptive neighbourhood selection and adaptive local-search depth control.

Two hybrid architectures are evaluated:Q-learning BF and DQN BF.

\subsubsection{Q-learning BF}
The Q-learning BF model combines the tabular Q-learning operator-selection policy with the BF selector responsible for FI/BI decisions.

Table~\ref{tab:qlearning_bf_summary} summarizes its global performance.
\input{../tables/qlearning_bf_summary.tex}

On small instances, the hybrid model remains relatively competitive, with an average gap of approximately +0.80\% relative to GVNS+.

The BF selector slightly stabilizes the exploration process by filtering some poor neighbourhood decisions generated by Q-learning. Several instances achieve solutions close to or better than GVNS+, and improvements exceeding 3\% are observed.

However, the additional decision layer also reduces some of the exploratory flexibility that makes Q-learning effective on smaller search spaces. Beneficial exploratory moves are occasionally suppressed, leading to noticeable degradations on some instances.

Runtime remains globally comparable to GVNS+, and several runs converge faster due to the combined guidance provided by both learning components.

As problem size increases, the limitations of the architecture become progressively more apparent.

Average degradation increases significantly from medium to large instances, eventually reaching substantial performance losses on the largest benchmark problems.

This behaviour indicates that the two learning mechanisms do not complement each other effectively at scale. As the state space grows, tabular Q-learning becomes increasingly unreliable because many states are visited too infrequently for stable value estimation. The BF selector is then forced to operate on neighbourhood choices that are already noisy or suboptimal.

Instead of correcting poor decisions, the selector may inadvertently reinforce them because both components depend on related search information. Consequently, hybridisation amplifies instability rather than improving robustness.

The degradation becomes particularly severe on large dynamic search spaces, where early neighbourhood decisions strongly influence the remainder of the optimization trajectory. Small prediction errors rapidly accumulate and may direct the search toward poor exploration regions.

Runtime behaviour reflects this instability. Some runs terminate early because the search prematurely converges toward poor local optima, while others consume nearly the entire computational budget.

Overall, the results show that combining tabular Q-learning with the BF selector does not overcome the scalability limitations of tabular reinforcement learning.

\subsubsection{DQN BF}
The DQN BF model combines the deep reinforcement learning controller with the supervised FI/BI selector.

Table~\ref{tab:dqn_bf_summary} summarizes the global performance of the hybrid architecture.
\input{../tables/dqn_bf_summary.tex}

On small and medium-scale instances, DQN BF exhibits balanced and stable behaviour. Solution quality remains close to GVNS+, with only moderate gaps and a limited number of significant degradations.

For small instances, the model behaves similarly to standalone DQN. The BF selector primarily acts as a stabilizing mechanism, reducing variability without significantly altering overall performance. Since small search spaces do not strongly benefit from neural generalization, the hybridization improves consistency more than optimization capability.

For medium-sized instances, this stabilizing effect becomes more pronounced. The BF selector filters part of the exploration noise generated by the DQN policy, reducing extreme behaviours and producing more regular search trajectories.

However, this additional control also limits aggressive exploration, preventing the model from exploiting some promising trajectories occasionally discovered by standalone DQN. Consequently, performance remains close to GVNS+ rather than consistently surpassing it.
Execution times remain competitive despite the additional learning components.

The strongest performance of DQN BF appears on large-scale instances. On 200-customer benchmarks, the model already demonstrates strong robustness, with most gaps remaining very small and frequently favourable. Improvements become increasingly systematic, while degradations remain limited.

On 400-customer instances, the hybrid architecture reaches its best overall performance. Results become remarkably consistent across nearly all benchmark instances, with the majority of gaps being negative or extremely close to zero.

Unlike the instability observed with Q-learning BF, the interaction between DQN and the BF selector becomes genuinely complementary at this scale.

This behaviour can be explained by the characteristics of large search spaces. The DQN component provides effective global guidance by generalizing across a massive state space, while the BF selector refines local-search behaviour by filtering noisy FI/BI choices and avoiding inefficient exploration phases.

Rather than conflicting, both learning mechanisms operate at different decision levels and reinforce each other. DQN determines which neighbourhood should be explored, whereas the BF selector determines how deeply that neighbourhood should be investigated.

Runtime behaviour also becomes increasingly favourable. Although large instances remain computationally demanding, the hybrid model often reaches competitive solutions within budgets comparable to GVNS+, while reducing unnecessary neighbourhood exploration.

Overall, DQN BF constitutes the most successful hybrid architecture evaluated in this work. The combination of deep reinforcement learning and supervised FI/BI selection preserves the scalability of DQN while improving search stability, resulting in a robust adaptive optimization framework for large VRPTW-S instances.

\subsection{Parameter control}

\subsubsection{PPO training diagnostics and convergence analysis}
Before evaluating the PPO controller on unseen benchmark instances, it is important to analyse the stability and convergence behaviour of the training process itself. Since the controller is learned offline from GVNS+ trajectories, the quality of the learned policy depends on the stability of the critic, the evolution of the exploration behaviour, and the consistency of the advantage estimates used during optimisation.

The diagnostics presented in this section focus on these complementary aspects in order to verify that the PPO controller converges toward a stable policy while maintaining sufficient exploration and reliable value estimation.

\paragraph{Global training stability:}
The first diagnostics evaluate the global stability of the PPO optimisation process. In PPO, two indicators are particularly informative: the critic loss, which measures how accurately the value network predicts future returns, and the policy entropy, which measures the degree of stochasticity of the learned policy.

A stable PPO training process should exhibit a critic loss that decreases and then stabilises, together with a progressive reduction of entropy rather than a sudden collapse toward a deterministic policy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../figures/ppo_training_diagnostics.png}
\caption{Evolution of critic loss and policy entropy during PPO training}
\label{fig:ppo_training_diagnostics}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_training_diagnostics}, the critic loss decreases rapidly during the first training epochs, dropping from approximately 0.25 to around 0.08 after roughly twenty epochs. After this initial learning phase, the loss stabilises within a narrow interval between approximately 0.08 and 0.10.

This behaviour is expected in PPO training. The rapid decrease observed during the early epochs indicates that the critic quickly learns a meaningful approximation of the long-term return associated with the search states. The subsequent stabilisation suggests that the value network reaches a reliable representation without divergence or oscillatory behaviour.

The slight increase occasionally observed after the stabilisation phase does not indicate instability. In reinforcement learning, particularly in PPO with entropy regularisation, small oscillations naturally occur because the policy continues exploring different actions while the critic adapts to the evolving policy distribution. The critic therefore remains stable while continuing to adjust to the progressively specialised policy.

The entropy curve exhibits a smooth and progressive decrease throughout training, from approximately 2.05 during the first epochs to around 0.55 at the end of training. Initially, entropy remains high because the controller explores many different parameter-adaptation actions with near-uniform probabilities. As training progresses, entropy decreases gradually, indicating increasing confidence regarding which actions are appropriate in different optimisation contexts.

Importantly, entropy never collapses to zero. Such a collapse would indicate premature convergence toward a deterministic policy and insufficient exploration. Instead, the observed behaviour demonstrates that the PPO controller preserves exploration during training while progressively specialising its decision-making process.

Taken together, the evolution of critic loss and entropy indicates a stable PPO optimisation process with no evidence of divergence, critic collapse, or premature policy determinism.

\paragraph{Critic and advantage analysis:}
Beyond global convergence indicators, additional diagnostics are required to assess whether the critic provides meaningful value estimates and whether the advantage signals used during optimisation remain well behaved.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../figures/ppo_critic_predictions.png}
\caption{Critic predictions versus empirical returns}
\label{fig:ppo_critic_predictions}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_critic_predictions} compares critic predictions with empirical discounted returns computed from the training trajectories. Each point corresponds to a sampled search state. The horizontal axis represents the value predicted by the critic network, while the vertical axis represents the empirical discounted return observed in the corresponding trajectory.

Most points remain concentrated around the diagonal, indicating strong agreement between predicted values and observed returns. The correlation coefficient between predictions and returns reaches approximately 0.90, confirming that the critic successfully captures the global structure of the return function and provides informative estimates of long-term search quality.

Some dispersion remains visible, particularly for larger return values. This behaviour is expected because the optimisation process itself remains stochastic. Similar search states may lead to slightly different future improvements depending on neighbourhood outcomes and subsequent search dynamics. Consequently, perfect prediction is neither expected nor necessary. PPO only requires a sufficiently informative baseline capable of reducing variance in policy updates.

Overall, the critic predictions remain coherent and stable enough to support reliable advantage estimation throughout training.

The final diagnostic concerns the distribution of the normalised advantages used during PPO optimisation.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../figures/ppo_advantages_distribution.png}
\caption{Distribution of normalised advantages during PPO training}
\label{fig:ppo_advantages_distribution}
\end{figure}

In PPO, the advantage measures whether the selected action performs better or worse than expected according to the critic. Positive advantages increase the probability of the selected action, whereas negative advantages reduce it.

Before optimisation, advantages are normalised by subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation. This normalisation stabilises gradient updates and prevents excessively large policy gradients.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_advantages_distribution}, the resulting distribution remains centred around zero and exhibits a smooth bell-shaped structure without extreme outliers or significant asymmetry. This behaviour indicates that critic predictions remain globally consistent with observed returns, policy gradients remain numerically stable, and no subset of transitions dominates the optimisation process.

The absence of extreme advantage values is particularly important because excessively large advantages may destabilise the clipped PPO objective and produce erratic policy updates. The observed distribution therefore confirms that the critic and policy remain well balanced during training and that PPO operates within a stable optimisation regime.

Overall, the training diagnostics indicate that the PPO controller converges toward a stable and meaningful parameter-adaptation policy. The critic successfully approximates long-term returns, entropy evolution confirms progressive policy specialisation without premature collapse, and the advantage distribution remains stable throughout optimisation.

The PPO controller demonstrates strong overall behaviour on small-scale instances. The average gap relative to GVNS+ remains negative, indicating a global improvement in solution quality, while the best observed improvement reaches approximately $-4.29\%$.

These results suggest that the learned policy successfully identifies effective search-control behaviours for relatively compact optimisation spaces. The controller dynamically adapts diversification and intensification according to the current search trajectory, allowing the search to escape local optima more efficiently than a fixed parameter configuration.

On small instances, this flexibility is particularly effective because promising regions of the search space can be exploited rapidly once identified. Consequently, the PPO controller achieves a more effective balance between exploration and exploitation than the baseline search process.

From a computational perspective, the runtime ratio remains close to the baseline. Although some runs become slightly longer due to additional diversification phases, others converge faster toward high-quality solutions, resulting in an overall runtime behaviour comparable to GVNS+.

The behaviour on medium-scale instances becomes more contrasted. The average gap remains favourable relative to GVNS+, and several instances exhibit substantial improvements, with gains reaching approximately $-4.07\%$.

However, the variability of the results increases compared with the small-instance setting. Since the policy is trained to maximise long-term expected reward, it occasionally favours exploration-oriented decisions that delay convergence in exchange for potentially larger future improvements.

As the search space grows, this behaviour produces a wider range of optimisation trajectories. Some runs benefit from the additional diversification, whereas others temporarily sacrifice exploitation efficiency. This trade-off explains the larger performance dispersion observed at this scale.

The runtime behaviour reflects this increased exploration activity. Diversification phases tend to be maintained for longer periods before convergence occurs, leading to a slightly higher runtime ratio. Nevertheless, the additional computational cost remains moderate relative to the overall optimisation budget.

For the 200-customer instances, the PPO controller continues to produce competitive results and preserves an overall negative average gap relative to GVNS+. The best observed improvement reaches approximately $-4.41\%$, demonstrating that the learned policy remains capable of identifying advantageous search strategies even within substantially larger search spaces.

At this scale, parameter adaptation becomes increasingly important because the search process must balance exploration and exploitation over much longer trajectories and across significantly larger neighbourhood structures. The PPO controller contributes by dynamically adjusting search behaviour according to the current optimisation context.

Some degradations relative to the baseline nevertheless remain observable. In certain situations, the controller maintains exploration-oriented behaviour longer than necessary, limiting the exploitation of promising regions before the stopping criterion is reached. Despite this limitation, runtime remains very close to the baseline, demonstrating that the adaptive mechanism scales efficiently.

The results obtained on the 400-customer instances confirm this general trend. The controller continues to generate meaningful improvements, with gains reaching approximately $-3.83\%$, while maintaining average performance very close to GVNS+.

At this scale, adaptive diversification becomes particularly valuable. The controller dynamically modifies shaking intensity and acceptance behaviour according to stagnation and improvement trends, helping the search avoid premature convergence in highly multimodal optimisation landscapes. Although result variability naturally increases with problem size, the PPO controller consistently remains competitive.

Moreover, runtime remains almost identical to GVNS+, indicating that the reinforcement learning control mechanism introduces only negligible overhead relative to the overall optimisation effort required for very large instances.

\subsubsection{Analysis of the learned parameter-control policy}

To better understand how the PPO controller modifies search behaviour in practice, its decisions were analysed on instance \texttt{C206\_100\_10}, where PPO achieved a gap of $-4.07\%$ relative to GVNS+.

Clustered instances are particularly informative because they contain numerous locally attractive routing configurations with similar objective values. Such structures frequently trap the search inside high-quality local optima corresponding to specific customer-cluster assignments. Consequently, effective optimisation requires a careful balance between diversification and intensification.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../figures/ppo_action_frequency.png} 
\caption{Action frequency distribution for instance C206\_100\_10}
\label{fig:ppo_action_frequency}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_action_frequency} presents the overall action distribution selected by the controller during optimisation.

The most frequently selected actions correspond to diversification behaviours. On this clustered instance, the controller deliberately injects larger shaking amplitudes and looser acceptance criteria in order to escape local optima and explore alternative cluster assignments. Strong diversification enables the search to jump between competing solution basins, while mild diversification provides a more controlled exploration mechanism capable of escaping shallow local minima without excessively disrupting promising structures.

The second most frequent group of actions corresponds to intensification. Once promising regions have been identified, the controller progressively reduces shaking amplitudes and tightens acceptance criteria in order to refine route structures and improve solution quality.

Actions associated with feasibility pressure are selected less frequently, indicating that time-window and synchronisation constraints are generally handled effectively by the standard penalty mechanism. Additional feasibility pressure therefore becomes necessary only in specific situations.

The neutral action acts as a stabilisation mechanism by preserving the current parameter configuration whenever neither intensification nor diversification appears advantageous. The reset action is rarely selected and is used only after prolonged stagnation periods.

The temporal evolution of these decisions provides additional insight.

\begin{figure}[ht]

Figure~\ref{fig:ppo_action_evolution} displays the sequence of actions selected throughout the optimisation process.

During the early search phase (approximately iterations 0--2500), strong and mild diversification dominate. The controller actively explores multiple regions of the search space by increasing shaking amplitudes and relaxing acceptance thresholds. This behaviour supports broad exploration and facilitates the construction of diverse routing structures.

During the intermediate phase (approximately iterations 2500--4000), diversification gradually decreases while intensification actions become increasingly frequent. The controller still triggers occasional diversification episodes whenever improvement rates decline, but the overall search behaviour progressively shifts toward exploitation.

Finally, during the late search phase (approximately iterations 4000--5500), strong intensification becomes dominant. The search focuses on fine-grained route refinement using smaller perturbations and stricter acceptance criteria. A single reset action is observed around iteration 5000 after a prolonged stagnation period. Following this reset, the search successfully escapes stagnation and produces the final solution improvement.

Overall, the observed behaviour confirms that the PPO controller learns a meaningful adaptive parameter-control policy. Rather than relying on fixed search parameters, it dynamically adjusts diversification, intensification, feasibility pressure, and restart behaviour according to the current optimisation context. This adaptive control mechanism enables the search to react to changing optimisation conditions and contributes directly to the competitive performance observed across all benchmark sizes.
